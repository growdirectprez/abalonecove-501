1976 Palos Verdes Properties Development Pressure — The 50-Year Pattern

1976 Palos Verdes Properties Development Pressure — The 50-Year Pattern

The first documented post-incorporation development pressure on RPV — only 10 months after RPV's first General Plan was adopted (June 26, 1975). The same playbook now playing out at 0 Clipper Road in 2024 was running in 1976. Same entity type (PVP = developer + landowner), same density-over-GP ask, same litigation-threat leverage, same lone-dissenter council dynamic, same resident-association pushback.

This article is the proof that the cycle is not new — and the framing for why the COAC reactivation framework is designed to break it.

What Happened (April 22, 1976 — Palos Verdes Peninsula News)

  • Palos Verdes Properties (PVP, successor to PV Project / post-Great-Lakes-Carbon era) submits formal application to develop:
  • 507 acres east and north of Portuguese Bend (largest parcel; explicitly contains the landslide area) — proposal: 389 units / 1.4 du per buildable acre (40% over GP)
  • 145 acres at the southeast corner of the city — proposal: 216 units / 2 du per buildable acre (38% over GP zoning of 156 units permitted)
  • Plus a 5-acre commercial area at PV Drive South + La Rotunda as a side ask
  • Original proposal had been 328 units; PVP came down to negotiate
  • D. Loring Marlett (PVP general manager) framed it as "reasonable and appropriate, does not involve significant change to the General Plan"
  • John P. Pollock (PVP attorney) framed the litigation threat: "If we can have the zone and plan changes... we could resolve this without going to litigation" — but "the company was not completely abandoning its options to litigate, and in that connection would soon file a claim with the city for damages to protect its rights"
  • Arthur Maie (Palos Verdes South Homeowners Association director) raised concerns about earlier plans for "three-story buildings and multiple units"; conditioned support on single-family character
  • Councilman Robert Ryan opposed any further study and asked that the proposal be rejected
  • Council majority approved the staff study anyway; report-back date set for May 4, 1976

The Landslide Tell

The 1976 article is most striking on this point: Palos Verdes Properties told the council the largest parcel "contains the landslide area" but counted it as having 281 buildable acres anyway and proposed building 389 units on it. This is exactly the logic the city itself has now reversed under state pressure — for 30+ years the city's own Housing Elements (1990, 2001) said the moratorium area was "Not buildable." The 2024 0 Clipper rezoning walks that position back. The developer in 1976 was already arguing the position the city itself adopted in 2024.

The geology was already settled science by 1976 — see Kerr & Drew 1967 (USAF/Columbia, 130 homes already lost, divided ownership identified as the structural obstacle). PVP knew. They proposed to build anyway. Pattern.

Why This Strengthens the COAC Argument

The COAC reactivation framework rests on the proposition that the existing public framework — General Plan + zoning + Housing Elements + Coastal Specific Plan — has been structurally insufficient to defend the area against developer pressure for 50 years. The 1976 article is empirical proof of that proposition. Adding a private-covenant authority layer (Declaration 100/101 reactivated through Article II §5; see coac declaration 100 article ii section 5) is the missing structural element.

The user's question — "will we learn or what?" — is the right framing. Repeated city-level reversals over 50 years show the city framework is the wrong tool. The COAC framework adds the tool that's been missing.
